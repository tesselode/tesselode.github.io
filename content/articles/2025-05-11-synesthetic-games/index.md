## Intro

I'd like to draw attention to a kind of game that's rare, but near and dear to my heart: synesthetic games. I'll explain what they are, what techniques they commonly use, and how different games use these techniques to different effects.

## What are synesthetic games?

Synesthetic games connect visuals, audio, and interaction into a cohesive artistic expression. These three senses are used not just to portray a game world, but also to directly express feelings.

This term is named after synesthesia, a phenomenon where some people experience one sense in response to a different sensory input. For instance, one may think of a letter as having a certain color to it, or one may feel tingling when they hear certain sounds.

To identify a synesthetic game, look for ways in which one sense directly correlates with another. Some examples:

- The music changes when the player performs a certain action
- Visuals or level design are synced with the beat of the music

Some other more subjective criteria:

- The game has visuals and audio for its own sake, not just to represent a world or communicate gameplay information
- The game feels like it's "dancing" to its own music

## What isn't a synesthetic game?

### Every video game ever

In a first-person shooter, you may hold the trigger on your controller and hear gunshots and see muzzle flashes in response. So the three senses are linked, right? Well, by this logic, every video game is a synesthetic game. But in most games, your input influences a character in a world, and the visuals and sound represent that world. For this discussion, we're looking for games that connect visuals, audio, and input in more abstract ways to create unique artistic statements.

### Rhythm games

Rhythm games aren't _necessarily_ synesthetic (although they can be, as we'll see below). Just because you're pressing inputs in time with music doesn't mean the audio and visuals are responding in kind to create a feeling.
